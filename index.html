A SOCIAL EXPERIMENT IN ONEIDA, NEW YORK

In the nineteenth century there was a village called Oneida in New York State where a “family” of 300 members lived together in a large beautiful house where they shared everything.

A man named John Humphrey Noyes, and a small group of people moved there in 1848. They wanted a place where they could live according to their particular beliefs in their efforts to create a more equal society.

Today, this place is touristic and, like me, many visitors come because they had relatives among those 19th century dreamers. Others just want to see for themselves the building where this successful social group in American history lived. “I don’t know of anywhere else where you can live in a historical place,” said the director of the Oneida site. “It’s very unusual.”

The present owners share the building with guests who stay in large comfortably furnished bedrooms with private baths. There are eight guest rooms in the hotel area, and each guest pays $100 for a big bedroom, a simple breakfast and a private tour of the 10,300-square-meter building, which also contains 35 apartments.

The library and the building’s grounds are also open to guests, as well as several of the public rooms. The 170-year-old library, unchanged from the original construction, holds a rich collection of 19th century books and magazines, which learners used to study Latin, Greek, algebra and astronomy.

This place is open for everybody and it’s worth a visit.

WHEN CITIES MAKE ROOM FOR NATURE

For decades, urban planners treated green spaces as pleasant extras rather than essential infrastructure. That view is changing. As summers become hotter and intense rainfall more frequent, cities are discovering that trees, parks and restored wetlands can perform jobs traditionally assigned to expensive engineering projects.

In one neighborhood, residents helped turn an abandoned parking lot into a small public garden. The garden did not solve the city's environmental problems, but it provided shade, absorbed some rainwater and created a place where neighbors who had rarely spoken began working together. Its success encouraged officials to consider similar projects elsewhere.

However, greener neighborhoods do not automatically become fairer neighborhoods. Improved public spaces can attract investment and raise housing costs, sometimes forcing long-term residents to leave. Researchers therefore argue that environmental projects should be accompanied by policies that protect affordable housing and involve local communities in decisions.

Some critics question whether small gardens can make a meaningful difference to problems as large as climate change. Supporters respond that these projects should not replace wider environmental policies. Instead, they can complement them while giving residents visible evidence that collective action is possible.

Ultimately, the most successful initiatives may be those that measure progress not only in degrees of cooling or liters of rainwater retained, but also in who benefits from the changes and who gets to shape them.
